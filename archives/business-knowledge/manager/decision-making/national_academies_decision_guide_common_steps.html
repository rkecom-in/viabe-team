Common Steps for Decision-Making

The following is a set of steps commonly recommended for a good decision-making process.

  1. Clarify the decision. What is the decision at hand and how does it relate to other decisions? This is the time to define the problem and consider who should be involved.
  2. Gather the right people. The right people to involve include people with knowledge or expertise on the topic, those who will be tasked with implementation, and, critically, the people who will be affected by the decision.
  3. Establish the facts. Explicitly write out what you know and gather relevant data. Necessary data can be scattered across a variety of sources, some of which may be harder to access than others. Reach out to data owners early in the process. Check basic facts and assumptions in addition to hard data. It is easy to discount or overlook seemingly obvious information, but it can be very helpful to clearly state all assumptions and facts.
  4. Define the values. Be clear about whose values should be considered in the decision: your own, politiciansʼ, the organizationʼs, or key stakeholdersʼ. Find methods to gather input on what these entitiesʼ values are if you do not already know and consult any relevant industry ethics. Determine how different values will be weighted and prioritized.
  5. Develop alternatives. List out the different actions or solutions on the table. Create these alternatives iteratively with later steps to estimate consequences and evaluate trade-offs as new information is learned. This will help you develop more refined or hybrid alternatives that offer a better balance across objectives. Alternatives that perform poorly on key metrics should be eliminated from further consideration, and desirable elements from different alternatives can be combined to create new ones.
  6. Assess the alternatives. Use the best available evidence and critical thinking to describe the predicted consequences of the alternatives. This may include complex quantitative modeling of possible outcomes or obtaining expert judgement. Structuring tools and professional facilitation can help to expose errors of logic and reasoning and improve the consistency and transparency of choices. Because objectives often compete, you may need to balance them by making trade-offs.
  7. Clarify uncertainties. Confront uncertainty by weighing the likelihood of different outcomes and assessing their possible effects.
  8. Decide. From the top alternatives coming out of the assessment, get final input from close advisors and key stakeholders. It can be helpful to establish whether there is a consensus and, if not, whether something could or should be done about that. Be very clear with everyone involved when you have made a final decision.
  9. Implement. Every decision requires proper care and attention to see it through to fruition. Follow up with those connected to the decision to ensure the necessary follow-on actions are being taken and help navigate challenges that arise.
